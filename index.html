Why Supplement Capsules Sometimes Stick Together

Open a bottle of capsules and find them stuck together in a clump, or fused to the side of the bottle, and the first thought is usually that the product has gone bad. Sometimes it has. Sometimes it has not. Capsules stick together for several reasons, and knowing which one applies tells you whether the bottle is safe to finish or should be thrown away.

The most common reason is moisture. Gelatin capsules absorb water from the air, and once they do, their surfaces soften and fuse. This happens fastest in a humid bathroom, in a bottle that has been opened many times, or in a bottle whose seal was broken long ago. A capsule that has absorbed moisture may still contain its full dose — the contents are sealed inside, after all — but the capsule itself is weakened, and it may dissolve too quickly or too slowly in the stomach. A few stuck capsules in an otherwise normal bottle are usually a storage problem, not a manufacturing one.

The second reason is heat. Capsules stored above room temperature — in a car, near a radiator, on a sunny shelf — can soften and stick, especially softgels, whose outer shell is made of gelatin and glycerine. Glycerine is hygroscopic, which means it attracts water, and a softgel in a warm, damp place will absorb moisture, swell, and fuse with its neighbours. A bottle of softgels that has melted into a single mass has been stored badly, and the contents may have degraded as well. The dose is no longer reliable, and the bottle should be replaced.

The third reason is the contents themselves. Some ingredients are hygroscopic — they pull moisture out of the air and into the capsule. Vitamin C, some B vitamins, and many herbal extracts behave this way. A manufacturer that fills capsules with a hygroscopic powder without adding a desiccant, or without drying the powder properly first, will produce capsules that stick together within weeks of manufacture, even in good storage. In that case the sticking is a manufacturing problem, and it may affect every bottle from that batch.

There is also a rarer and more serious cause: contamination. A capsule that is sticky, discoloured, or smells unusual may have been exposed to moisture during manufacture, which can allow mould or bacteria to grow. A bottle with a sour or chemical smell, or with capsules that are spotted or cracked, should not be used. The risk is low, but it is real, and the cost of replacing a bottle is small compared with the cost of taking a contaminated product.

The practical advice is simple. Store capsules in a cool, dry place, keep the bottle tightly closed, and keep the silica packet inside until the bottle is finished. If a few capsules stick together but the rest are fine, the bottle is probably usable — but note the lot number and watch for further problems. If the whole bottle is fused, or if anything smells wrong, throw it away and tell the brand. A brand that hears about sticking capsules often enough will fix its packaging or its process, and the next batch will be better.
